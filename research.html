Current Research

Alternative Temporalities:
New Trajectories for Traditional Music Today

Since the late 2010s, artists who, in one way or another, draw on traditional music in their work have become increasingly prominent in the global scene of pop avant-garde and so-called independent music.

The Shift

Musicians of the fourth wave of the folk revival in the UK and Ireland, artists from the Global South, masters of oral classical music, and experimenters who are expanding the concept of regional traditions are becoming integrated into the infrastructure of festivals dedicated to experimental, electronic, extreme, or adventurous music.

In this context, these stylistically, regionally, and genre-diverse musical practices form, if not a unified body, then at least a diverse one. Moreover, they are presented there not as a separate, exotic component, but as a full-fledged part of the main program.

This conceptual shift encompasses not only major festivals but also small venues, community radio stations, independent labels, media outlets, and so on.

Research

In this study, we aim, first, to describe the shift that is taking place, and second, to critically examine its causes and how it is perceived in European and regional contexts.

Drawing on numerous interviews, fieldwork, and related studies (both scholarly and journalistic), we examine why, since the early 2010s, the market and cultural communities—which had previously been oriented toward avant-garde and experimental genres— began to take notice of traditional music outside the categories of “world music”; how the interest of Western institutions influences non-Western cultures and the language used to describe local phenomena; and how these genre shifts are directly linked to colonial and capitalist hierarchies.

Central Question

How do the various musical and sonic practices of different communities integrate into modernity, reject its totality, or create alternative temporalities?

Material
& Methods

For this study, we conducted more than 30 in-depth interviews with musicians, representatives of record labels, festivals, and booking agencies, as well as academic researchers, journalists, and archivists from various countries and regions: Ireland, England, Scotland, Germany, Indonesia, the North Caucasus, Pakistan, Poland, and others.

In addition, our research drew on an analysis of label catalogs, festival programs, and changes in the language used in press releases and other texts.

Interviews
More than 30 in-depth interviews with musicians, curators, labels, festivals, booking agents, researchers, journalists and archivists.
Geography
Ireland, England, Scotland, Germany, Indonesia, the North Caucasus, Pakistan, Poland and other regions.
Sources
Label catalogs, festival programs, press releases, media texts, fieldwork and related academic and journalistic research.

Theoretical
Framework

The theoretical framework for the research was drawn from the ideas and works of Steven Feld, Budhaditya Chattopadhyay, Mark Fisher, Thomas Burkhalter, Kerstin Klenke, Dipesh Chakrabarty, and other ethnomusicologists, anthropologists, decolonial thinkers, and even music journalists.

Disciplinary
Field

Disciplinarily, the research falls within the fields of ethnomusicology, social anthropology, and pop music studies, and addresses issues of power dynamics in culture, a critical approach to the concept of cultural heritage, identity politics, and (anti)colonial nationalism.

Planned
Outcome

The planned outcome of the research is a monograph and a series of conferences and concerts that combine theoretical and practical elements.
